ABOUT AERONX

Engineering a bridge between biology and mobility.

AeronX explores how biological systems can become part of the future of emission management.

Modern vehicles rely on sophisticated emission-control technologies, but carbon management remains a major engineering challenge.

AeronX approaches this challenge from a different direction: combining engineered gas pre-treatment with the natural carbon fixation capability of microalgae.

Our goal is not to replace existing emission-control systems, but to investigate how biological carbon capture could be integrated into a broader vehicle-emission management architecture.

THE CHALLENGE

Carbon management is a systems problem.

Capturing carbon from a vehicle exhaust stream is not simply a matter of putting algae in front of exhaust gas. Temperature, particulates, gas composition, mass transfer and biological conditions all have to be considered.

01 / GAS

Complex exhaust streams

Vehicle exhaust contains more than CO₂. A biological system therefore needs appropriate pre-treatment and conditioning.

02 / BIOLOGY

Living systems are sensitive

Microalgae depend on factors such as temperature, light, pH, nutrient availability and carbon transfer.

03 / ENGINEERING

Scale changes everything

Moving from a laboratory or prototype system to industrial gas flow introduces challenges involving pressure drop, materials, energy, maintenance and reactor scale.

THE SCIENCE

Biology with an engineering mindset.

AeronX is based on established principles of photosynthesis, gas–liquid mass transfer and environmental control.

Photosynthesis

Microalgae use light energy to convert inorganic carbon and water into organic compounds while releasing oxygen.

CO₂ + H₂O + light → biomass + O₂

Gas–liquid transfer

Carbon dioxide must transfer from the gas phase into the liquid culture before it can be utilized by the biological system. Gas dispersion and contact area therefore matter.

Biological conditions

Temperature, pH, light intensity, nutrients, biomass concentration and gas availability can all influence microalgal growth and carbon uptake.
